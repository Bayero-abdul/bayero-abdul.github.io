When it comes to building machine learning models, defining your validation and test sets is like choosing the right gear for an adventure — you need the right tools for the journey ahead. It might be tempting to just grab a random chunk of your data and call it a day, but hold on! This approach can lead you astray, especially when your data has quirks or complexities. In this post, we’ll explore how to wisely define your validation and test sets, with examples that will make the concepts stick like glue.

Why Not Just Split Randomly?

Imagine you’re baking a cake. You wouldn’t taste the batter from just one corner of the bowl and assume the whole thing is perfectly mixed, right? Similarly, randomly splitting your data without considering its nature can give you a skewed sense of your model’s performance.

For example, let’s say you’re building a model to predict the popularity of songs. You might think, “I’ll just randomly grab 20% of the data for validation.” But hold on! What if your data includes a mix of songs from different genres, decades, and artists? A random split might end up with all the 80s rock anthems in the training set and all the 2000s pop hits in the validation set. The result? A model that’s great at predicting one genre but flops on another.

Let’s explore some smarter strategies with a few colorful examples.

Example 1: Predicting the Weather — But with a Time Machine?

Suppose you’re developing a model to predict next week’s weather based on historical data. Your data spans from 2014 to 2024, and you’re itching to see how well your model performs. If you randomly split the data, you might end up with a training set that includes bits from both 2014 and 2024— and the same for your validation set.

Why This Is a Problem: Your model might learn to predict tomorrow’s weather by sneakily using tomorrow’s data that just happens to be in the training set. It’s like cheating on a test by peeking at the answers.

Better Approach: Instead of a random split, use a chronological split. Train your model on data from 2014 to 2023, and validate it on 2024. This way, you’re simulating a real-world scenario where you use past data to predict the future — just like you would in production.

Example 2: Detecting Fraud — But the Crooks Keep Getting Smarter

Imagine you’re building a fraud detection system for a bank. Your dataset includes thousands of transactions, with some flagged as fraudulent. However, fraudsters are constantly evolving their tactics. If you randomly split the data, your model might catch onto older patterns but miss out on the latest tricks.

Why This Is a Problem: Just like you wouldn’t use outdated antivirus software to protect your computer, you shouldn’t train your model on outdated fraud tactics. A random split could make your model too good at detecting old schemes, leaving it vulnerable to new ones.

Better Approach: Use a time-based split where you train on older data and validate on more recent transactions. This way, your model is always tuned to the latest patterns, ready to catch those sneaky fraudsters in the act.

Example 3: Identifying Rare Birds — But What If Some Fly South?

You’re building a model to identify bird species from photographs. Your dataset includes thousands of images, but some species are much rarer than others. If you randomly split the data, you might end up with only a few rare birds in the training set and none in the validation set — or vice versa.

Why This Is a Problem: If your model never sees a rare bird in the training set, it’ll have no idea how to recognize it in the validation set. Conversely, if it only sees rare birds in the training set, it might perform well on those but poorly on more common species.

Better Approach: Use stratified sampling to ensure that both your training and validation sets have a representative mix of common and rare species. This way, your model gets a balanced diet of data, learning to recognize all types of birds, from the everyday sparrow to the elusive golden eagle.

Conclusion: Use Judgment to Define Validation and Test Sets

The key takeaway is that the process of defining validation and test sets requires careful consideration of your specific problem domain. While random splitting is a good starting point, it’s not always sufficient. You need to think about how the data you will encounter in the future might differ from your current dataset and structure your validation and test sets accordingly.

By doing so, you can create a more robust evaluation of your model’s performance, leading to better generalization and ultimately, more reliable machine learning systems.

What’s Next?

Now that you have a solid understanding of how to define your validation and test sets, consider the specifics of your current project. Are there any unique characteristics of your data that might require special handling? Use the examples above as a guide, and tailor your approach to suit the needs of your particular model. Happy modeling!
